\section{The \savyar{} System}
\label{sec:system}

\input{figures/pipeline}

\savyar{} has two parts (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). A rule-based analyzer lists the analyses of every word in a sentence. A neural ranker reads the analyses of all words of the sentence and chooses one analysis per word. All knowledge of individual roots sits in the analyzer.

\subsection{Analyzer}
\label{sec:analyzer}

\paragraph{Lexicon.}
The analyzer looks up roots in a lexicon of 22,464 nominal and 1,054 verbal entries taken from \TBD{dictionary source}. A dictionary word that the suffix rules derive from a shorter root by its actual derivation is not an entry: \tr{bulun} `be found' is \tr{bul-un} and \tr{durum} `situation' is \tr{dur-um}. Words that only resemble a derivation remain roots (\tr{dolar} `dollar', \tr{gece} `night'), and so do words formed with a suffix the rules do not model (\tr{satıcı} `seller', \tr{öğretmen} `teacher'). The lexicon was built without consulting any treebank, so test sentences cannot influence which candidates exist.

\paragraph{Suffixes.}
The analyzer has 99 suffix types. Each records the category it attaches to and the category it produces: 40 map nouns to nouns, 34 verbs to nouns, 17 verbs to verbs and 2 nouns to verbs, while 6 attach to either category. Each suffix generates its surface allomorphs from the stem, following vowel harmony, consonant alternation and buffer consonants.

\paragraph{Search.}
Every prefix of a word is tried as a root, both as written and after undoing a stem alternation such as final-consonant voicing (\tr{kitab-ı} from \tr{kitap} `book'). From each root a depth-first search attaches suffixes whose allomorph matches the rest of the word. Suffixes fall into 13 ordered groups, from verb-to-verb derivation through negation, compound verbs, category-changing derivation, converbs, plural, possessive, case, the relative \tr{-ki}, the comitative \tr{-lA}, adverbial suffixes and predicative suffixes to person endings. A suffix may not follow a suffix of a later group. Derivational suffixes may follow each other, and the relative \tr{-ki} restarts the order, which licenses \tr{ev-de-ki-ler-den} house-\gl{loc}-\gl{rel}-\gl{pl}-\gl{abl} `from those in the house'.

\paragraph{Root-dependent rules.}
Because the ranker never sees the root, every rule that depends on it is applied here. Closed lists name the verbs that take the causatives \tr{-Ir} (\tr{geç-ir} `spend (time)') and \tr{-Ar} (\tr{çık-ar} `take out'), the 13 monosyllabic verbs whose aorist is \tr{-Ir} (\tr{gel-ir} `comes'), and the roots that lose their last vowel before a vowel-initial suffix (\tr{ağz-ı} `his or her mouth'). Pronoun restrictions are also enforced here: personal pronouns and the demonstratives \tr{bu} and \tr{şu} take no possessive, so \tr{bizi} is only `us' (\gl{acc}). When no lexicon root yields an analysis, as with many proper nouns and acronyms, the analyzer hypothesises a root from the surface form; the suffixes must still span the rest of the word.

\paragraph{Output.}
Each analysis is a root, the category of the root (noun or verb), a suffix sequence and the resulting category. On the TrMor2018 test words, the analyzer returns \TBD{cand-per-word} analyses per word, \TBD{amb-share}\% of words have more than one, and the gold analysis is among the candidates for \TBD{coverage}\% of words.

\subsection{Root-agnostic encoding}
\label{sec:encoding}

The ranker sees each candidate word as a short token sequence. The first token stands for the root: \tok{N} for any nominal root and \tok{V} for any verbal root, or a token of its own for one of 102 closed-class lexemes such as \tr{ben} `I', \tr{bu} `this', \tr{ve} `and', \tr{için} `for' and the question particle \tr{mI}. One token per suffix follows, identified by the suffix type. Every token carries its suffix group and its position in the word. The vocabulary has 212 entries: 99 suffixes, 102 lexemes, the two root tokens, four sentence-final punctuation marks and five special symbols.

Table~\ref{tab:encoding} shows what this representation keeps and what it discards. The three analyses of \tr{masalı} differ in their suffixes and remain distinct. The pronoun reading of \tr{bana} `to me' stays distinct from a reading built on the noun \tr{ban}, because \tr{ben} has a lexeme token. Analyses that differ only in an open-class root of the same category become identical: \tr{yaz-ın} `your summer' and \tr{yazı-n} `your writing' both encode as \tok{N}~\gl{poss.2sg}, and \tr{dol-ar} `it fills' and \tr{dola-r} `it wraps' both encode as \tok{V}~\gl{aor}. \subsection{Ranker}
\label{sec:ranker}

The ranker scores every analysis of every word of a sentence in one forward pass. It has three parts.

\paragraph{Analysis encoder.}
Each token of an analysis is embedded as the sum of a token embedding, an embedding of its suffix group and an embedding of its position in the word, all of 384 dimensions. A two-layer Transformer encoder \citep{vaswani-etal-2017-attention} contextualises the tokens of the analysis. The layer-normalised mean of their final states is the analysis vector $\mathbf{a}_{ij}$ of analysis $j$ of word $i$.

\paragraph{Sentence encoder.}
A word enters the sentence as the set of its analyses. The mean and the element-wise maximum of its analysis vectors are concatenated, projected to 384 dimensions, and added to an embedding of the word's position in the sentence. One further position after the last word holds the sentence-final punctuation mark, or an end symbol when there is none. A six-layer Transformer encoder turns these word vectors into context vectors $\mathbf{c}_i$. The context of a word is therefore built from all analyses of its neighbours, without committing to any of them.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
Analysis $j$ of word $i$ receives the score
\begin{equation}
s_{ij} = \mathrm{MLP}\big([\mathbf{c}_i;\, \mathbf{a}_{ij};\, \mathbf{c}_i \odot \mathbf{a}_{ij}]\big),
\end{equation}
where MLP is a two-layer perceptron with a GELU activation. A softmax over the analyses of word $i$ gives $p(j \mid i)$, and the prediction for each word is its highest-scoring analysis. No search over sentences is needed.

All Transformer layers use pre-layer normalisation, eight attention heads, a feed-forward width of 1,536, GELU activations and dropout 0.1. The model has 15.2 million parameters, 10.6 million of them in the sentence encoder.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}

\paragraph{Objective.}
The loss is the cross-entropy of the gold analysis $g_i$, averaged over the set $A$ of ambiguous words in a batch:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{i \in A} \log p(g_i \mid i).
\end{equation}
Unambiguous words take part in the context of their neighbours but carry no loss of their own. When the analyzer cannot reach the gold analysis of a word, the gold suffix sequence is encoded directly and the analyzer is run again with the annotated root to obtain alternatives.

\paragraph{Synthetic ambiguity.}
More than half of the training words have a single analysis and so carry no loss. The root abstraction lets them be used. \tr{Kapıyı} `the door (\gl{acc})' has the single analysis \tok{N}~\gl{acc}, while \tr{kitabı} has the analyses \tok{N}~\gl{acc} and \tok{N}~\gl{poss.3sg}. Given the analyses of \tr{kitabı}, \tr{kapıyı} becomes an example that the ranker cannot tell apart from a real occurrence of \tr{kitabı}. Only the context can resolve it, and its label is known because it is the word's own analysis.

We call the set of encodings of a real ambiguous word its class $K$. Let $n(K,e)$ be the number of ambiguous training words of class $K$ whose gold analysis has encoding $e$, $n(e) = \sum_K n(K,e)$, and $u(e)$ the number of unambiguous training words with encoding $e$. Synthetic examples of a class must follow the label distribution of its real examples, so every gold encoding of the class has to supply them in proportion. With a scale $\lambda$, let $\rho(e) = \min\big(1, u(e) / (\lambda\, n(e))\big)$ and let $\rho(K)$ be the minimum of $\rho(e)$ over the encodings $e$ with $n(K,e) > 0$. An unambiguous word with encoding $e$ is given the analyses of class $K$ with probability
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda\, \rho(K)\, n(K,e)}{u(e)}.
\end{equation}
Class $K$ then receives $\lambda\, \rho(K)\, n(K,e)$ synthetic examples labelled $e$ in expectation, the same label distribution as its real examples. A class whose gold encodings never occur as unambiguous words receives none, and classes in which two analyses share an encoding are not used. The draws are repeated for every batch, so each epoch sees different synthetic examples. With $\lambda = 1$, a training split of TrMor2018 gains about 39,000 synthetic examples per epoch in addition to its 139,000 ambiguous words.

\paragraph{Optimisation.}
We train with AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01) on batches of 32 sentences, with 500 warm-up steps followed by cosine decay to 5\% of the peak rate, gradient clipping at norm 1.0 and mixed precision.
